Human-in-the-loop is a deliverable, not a safety blanket

Almost everyone selling automation says "and then a person reviews it." Very few can say who reviews, at which step, what's on their screen, how many clicks it takes to agree and how many to object, and what happens to the queue when that person is on holiday. A review step nobody designed becomes a rubber stamp within a couple of weeks. It deserves the same design effort as the automation itself. Here's how we do it in two of our systems.

Why review turns into rubber-stamping

The cause is almost always the interface, not the people. If agreeing takes one click and objecting takes five, within two weeks everyone agrees by default. If the system shows a finding but not the document behind it, reviewers have to open files separately, and soon they stop. If a warning is always on, nobody reads it.

Hence a simple rule: the quality of review isn't decided by whether a person is in the loop. It's decided by how their screen is built.

Five questions a review step has to answer

  1. What reaches a person, and what doesn't?
  2. What do they see when they decide?
  3. Which decisions are possible, and what does each one cost?
  4. What gets recorded afterwards?
  5. What happens when there's nobody to review?

The answers below come from two systems: Kestrel checks supplier invoices, Osprey checks per-person document folders for staff and clients.

1. What reaches a person

In Osprey, a folder is marked "Complete" only when the system is sure. Anything it can't establish firmly — an unreadable page, a date it can't find, a file in an unexpected place — marks the folder "Needs review" and sends it to a person.

That's a deliberate asymmetry. An unnecessary flag costs a reviewer five minutes. A false "all clear" means an inspector finds the expired certificate first.

In numbers: on the second test batch, generated after the system was frozen, 2 findings went to a person and there were 4 false alarms. Not one silent miss. Every mistake the system made became either extra work for a reviewer or a question for them, never a gap.

2. What the reviewer sees

Both systems use the same layout: the document on the left, the findings on the right. The reviewer doesn't go hunting for files; they look at what the system looked at.

In Osprey, that's literally the same page images the model read. If the model misread something, the reviewer sees it immediately.

In Kestrel, findings are grouped by invoice line, because that's how people read a document. One line often carries two findings — a total that doesn't add up and a price above contract are usually the same mistake seen twice. Grouping stops the reviewer deciding the same thing twice, while the database still stores each finding separately.

3. Which decisions exist, and what they cost

Confirm takes one click. Reject requires a reason from a fixed list. Kestrel has six:

ReasonWhat it means
MisreadThe system took the wrong value
Stale contractThe price is right; our contract is out of date
Agreed separatelyArranged with the supplier outside the contract
Within toleranceThe difference is too small to pursue
Already countedSame problem, decided on another finding
OtherExplain in your own words — not accepted without text

Why a list rather than a free-text box? Because a list can be counted. If half the rejections a month from now say "stale contract," the problem isn't your suppliers. It's your contracts.

Osprey adds a third decision, correct: the reviewer types in the fact printed on the page, such as an issue date, and the expiry is recalculated by the system's code, not by the reviewer in their head.

4. What gets recorded

Every decision lands in two places: the finding's current state, and an event log. The log is append-only, enforced by the database itself. It keeps what the finding looked like before the decision, and who made it. A decision can be read months later, even if the finding has since been decided again.

Two more rules:

  • A finding closed by an automatic rule can't be quietly re-decided; otherwise the reason it closed would be lost.
  • In Osprey, decisions are stored beside the findings, never inside them. A reviewer can't "improve" the system's measured accuracy by deciding. The 219 out of 220 is the system's work, not the people who came after it.

5. Warnings people actually read

Osprey warns about expiring documents in tiers: 90, 60 and 30 days ahead. But a care plan reviewed every six months would sit inside a 90-day tier for half its life. So tiers are set per document type, and for documents like that they're 30 and 14 days. A warning that's always on stops being read.

What our systems don't do yet

To be straight about it: neither Kestrel nor Osprey currently tells anyone that the review queue is growing and the reviewer hasn't shown up for a week. We settle that at deployment, with each client: who covers for the reviewer, after how many days the queue escalates, and to whom. If your automation has no answer to that question, it has no review step — only the hope that someone is at their desk.

What to ask anyone who promises "a person reviews it"

  1. Show me the review screen. Is the document itself on it?
  2. How many clicks does it take to reject a finding, and do I have to give a reason?
  3. What reaches a person automatically, and what does the system decide on its own?
  4. Where are decisions recorded, and can they be changed after the fact?
  5. What happens when the reviewer is on holiday?

Both systems were tested on synthetic data; every name and document is fictional. The code for Kestrel's review screen is open on GitHub; Osprey's architecture is described in its repository.
