The EU AI Act for a five-person company: what you actually have to do

If your business uses AI tools rather than building them, the EU AI Act asks surprisingly little of you: tell people when they're talking to a machine, take reasonable steps to make sure your team understands the tools, and stay away from a short list of banned practices. The heavy obligations only start where AI helps decide things about people — hiring, credit, access to services — and for most of those systems they don't apply until 2 December 2027.

Here's what is already in force, what is coming, and what you can safely skip.

First, work out which role you play

The Act sorts businesses into two main roles. A provider builds an AI system and puts it out under its own name. A deployer uses someone else's system in its own work.

A bakery that switches on a ready-made chatbot from a vendor is a deployer. But if you built your own assistant on the OpenAI or Anthropic API and put it on your website, you are most likely the provider of that assistant. That matters, because the duty to tell visitors they're talking to AI sits with the provider.

The timeline

DateWhat applies
2 February 2025Prohibited practices; AI literacy
2 August 2026Transparency rules for chatbots, AI-generated content and deepfakes (Article 50)
2 December 2026Marking of AI-generated content for generative systems already on the market before August 2026; new prohibitions
2 December 2027High-risk rules for stand-alone systems: hiring, credit, education and more
2 August 2028High-risk rules for AI built into regulated products

The 2027 date is new. High-risk obligations were originally due in August 2026, but the EU pushed them back with the Digital Omnibus on AI, which entered into force in July 2026. The transparency rules were not delayed: they have applied since 2 August 2026, with only narrow parts deferred.

Obligation 1: your chatbot has to say it's a bot

This is the one that touches almost every small business with AI on its website. Under the Commission's July 2026 guidelines on Article 50, providers of interactive systems must tell people clearly, before the conversation starts, that they are dealing with an AI. There is an exception when the AI nature is obvious to an average user, but betting your compliance on "it was obvious" is a poor trade for a one-line fix.

In practice: the bot's first message says "I'm an AI assistant, not a human," and the chat window header says the same. Five minutes of work. Our own assistant, Neo, opens every conversation exactly that way.

Obligation 2: AI literacy for your team

This has applied since February 2025, but the Omnibus softened it. Article 4 now asks providers and deployers to take measures that support AI literacy among the people using AI on their behalf, and it no longer requires you to guarantee a particular level of knowledge for each individual.

For a five-person team, reasonable measures look like this: a one-page internal policy — which AI tools we use, what for, and what never goes into them — and an hour where everyone reads and discusses it. Keep the date. That's your evidence.

Obligation 3: labelling AI content — only in specific cases

You don't need to label everything AI touched. Labelling applies to deepfakes and to AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest. A realistic "photo" of a person who doesn't exist in your ad is a deepfake, so label it. A social post about your new menu drafted with ChatGPT is not.

When AI helps decide about people, the rules change

Hiring and candidate screening, employee evaluation, creditworthiness, access to education and essential services: these are high-risk uses. The Act's employment category covers recruitment, selection, targeted job ads, candidate evaluation and performance monitoring.

If you deploy a system like that, from December 2027 Article 26 requires you to use it according to its instructions, with competent human oversight, to monitor how it performs, to keep its logs for at least six months, and to inform workers and their representatives before using it in the workplace.

Not every HR tool is automatically high-risk. A system that only performs a narrow procedural task — such as moving data from CVs into a table without ranking anyone — may fall under an exception. But that is an assessment you make and write down, not an assumption.

What probably doesn't apply to you

  • Obligations for general-purpose AI model makers. That's OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and similar companies.
  • Conformity assessments, quality management systems, EU database registration. Those fall on providers of high-risk systems.
  • The prohibited practices: social scoring, manipulative techniques, emotion recognition at work. If you've never considered doing these, you almost certainly aren't.

GDPR still applies

The AI Act sits alongside GDPR, not instead of it. If your AI tools process personal data about customers or staff, you still need to know which vendors process that data and why, and fully automated decisions with significant effects on people remain restricted. A single page listing your AI vendors and what they do covers part of your GDPR homework and answers most of the questions a careful client will ask.

A one-day checklist

  1. List every AI tool your team uses.
  2. Mark where AI talks to customers directly, and add a clear "this is AI" notice there.
  3. Write a one-page AI use policy and spend an hour on it with the team.
  4. Check whether AI plays any part in hiring, credit or access decisions. If it does, you have until December 2027 — but start now.
  5. Write down which vendors process data, and for what.

This is an orientation guide, not legal advice. If you're unsure whether your use case is high-risk, that question alone is worth a conversation with a lawyer.
