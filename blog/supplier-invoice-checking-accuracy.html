How we check supplier invoices — and how we know what we miss

Almost every invoice checking system tells you how many discrepancies it found. None tell you how many it missed. We built ours the other way round: first a batch of 260 documents with 98 errors planted on purpose and a ledger of the right answers, then the checking pipeline, measured against that ledger. Result: 94 errors found and named correctly, 2 found but named differently, and 2 more stopped at the door as exact copies. The data is synthetic, and that is exactly why the number can be precise.

Why "we found 14 overcharges" tells you nothing

Picture the report: "14 invoices with inflated prices found this month." Sounds convincing. But was that 14 out of 15, or 14 out of 60? A missed invoice leaves no trace. It just gets paid.

Answering that question means knowing the right answer in advance. With real documents, nobody does. If they did, the checking would be unnecessary.

How the experiment works

We built a batch of 260 documents: 248 invoices and 12 duplicates, in three different layouts. Some are clean text PDFs; the rest are scans of three kinds: tidy scans, worn scans and phone photos.

98 errors were planted across 90 documents, and every one was written into a ledger: which document, which line, which field, what the invoice says and what it should say.

The ledger is walled off by database permissions. The checking pipeline physically cannot read it. That is enforced by PostgreSQL, not by good intentions.

Eight kinds of error

ErrorWhat it means in practice
Price above contractThe supplier charged more than agreed
Stale price listThe invoice uses last period's prices
Unit mismatchPriced per kilo, delivered per piece, or the other way round
Unknown itemA product on the invoice that isn't in the contract
Missing discountThe volume crossed a discount threshold, but no discount was applied
Wrong VAT rateThe rate doesn't match the product
Duplicate invoiceThe same invoice arrived twice
Arithmetic errorQuantity × price ≠ line total, or the totals don't add up

Duplicates come in three kinds, in rising order of difficulty: an exact copy of the file, the same invoice rebuilt from scratch, and the sneaky one — same supplier, same date, same total, but a new invoice number. That last kind can only be flagged as a suspicion, not proven, so it goes to a person.

The two "misses" are exact copies of earlier invoices. They're rejected at intake by file fingerprint and never reach reconciliation at all. That's not a gap; it's the first line of defence doing its job earlier.

Three rules that made the result possible

The model reads, the code counts. The AI model extracts what it sees on the page and points to the exact fragment each value came from. Not one addition and not one price-list comparison is left to it. That's not distrust; it's reproducibility. A run you can't repeat with the same result measures nothing.

Prices are looked up by delivery date, not invoice date. Some April invoices pay for March deliveries. Check them against the price on the invoice date, and an entire class of error — billing from a stale price list — becomes invisible.

The original is never altered. The system doesn't correct a supplier's invoice. It shows the discrepancy to a person, who decides whether to ask the supplier for a corrected document. Rewriting someone else's document destroys the audit trail.

What went wrong along the way

We built this in public and wrote down the mistakes alongside the decisions. The extraction instructions had to be rewritten twice:

  • Version 2. Some invoices group their lines by delivery, and each group has its own date. Version 1 took one date for the whole invoice, so some lines were checked against the wrong price list.
  • Version 3. In the "invoice to" block, the legal entity and the specific site — a restaurant, a warehouse — are different things. Version 2 sometimes blended them, and invoices were assigned to the wrong site.

Both problems surfaced only because a ledger of right answers existed. In an ordinary system, they would have lived quietly for years.

What this number doesn't mean

94 out of 98 is a result on a synthetic batch with layouts we know. Your invoices look different, and your suppliers make their own kinds of mistakes. The real accuracy on your documents is unknown until it's measured. That's why we start with a pilot, not a promise.

Five questions for anyone selling automated invoice checking

  1. How many errors does your system miss, and how did you measure that?
  2. Are prices checked against the invoice date or the delivery date?
  3. Does an AI model do the arithmetic, or ordinary code?
  4. Does the system correct the invoice itself, or show the discrepancy to a person?
  5. How do you catch a duplicate invoice that comes back with a new number?

If the first question gets no answer with a number in it, what you're being offered is a counter of findings, not a check.
