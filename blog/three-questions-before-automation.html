Three questions we ask before writing a single node

Who notices when this breaks? How will we know it worked? What does the business do on the day it's switched off? If any of the three has no answer, it's too early to build. Here's what the questions mean in practice, and how we answer them in our own systems — including one place where our first answer was weak.

1. Who notices when this breaks?

Automation rarely fails loudly. A supplier changes their email format, an access token expires, Google returns an empty file list. The worst case isn't an error. It's "nothing happened," which looks exactly like "everything's fine."

Our first answer was weak. In Kestrel, our invoice checking system, n8n errors go to a separate workflow that writes them to the database. The error is on record, but nobody hears about it until someone goes looking. That's half an answer.

In Osprey, we fixed it. Every workflow reports failures to an error-handling workflow, which marks the run as failed and emails the operator what stopped, where, and what to do. A run counts as finished only when every stage has succeeded, and the database itself enforces that, not good intentions. An empty response from Google Drive is treated as an error, not as "no documents." A file in an unexpected place becomes a finding for a person, never a silent skip.

The rule we took away: "found nothing" and "couldn't look" must never look the same.

2. How will we know it worked?

Usually "it works" means "it doesn't crash." But a system that never crashes and misses half the errors is worse than none, because people trust it.

Our answer is measurement against a known answer. We plant errors in a test batch, record them in a ledger, and measure the system against it: 94 out of 98 for Kestrel, 219 out of 220 for Osprey. With a client, that's the pilot: a first run on their own documents, with a result they can check.

This question also decides how to price the work. If success can be measured, paying for results is a real option. If it can't, value-based pricing is guesswork, and hourly billing is the honest choice.

3. What does the business do on the day it's switched off?

This is the question people dislike. But an automation nobody can live without and nobody can operate is a liability dressed as an asset.

Our systems are built so that switching them off breaks nothing:

  • Read-only access. Osprey never renames, moves or edits anything in Google Drive. Kestrel never alters a supplier's invoice.
  • Originals stay where they were. Documents live exactly where they lived before the system arrived.
  • Reports are ordinary Google Sheets. They stay readable after the system stops.

And a rule for client work: the manual fallback is written down before we build, in the client's own words, and tested once. If the system stops tomorrow, someone has to know what to do on Monday morning.

If there's no answer

That doesn't mean the automation isn't worth doing. It means you start somewhere else:

QuestionBad answerWhere to start
Who notices when it breaks?"We'll see"Name a person and a channel: who gets the error message, and where
How will we know it worked?"Well, it runs"Pick one measurable number and record it before launch
What happens when it's switched off?"It won't be"Write the manual procedure and walk through it once

The three questions take half an hour of conversation. Skipping them costs months.

Kestrel and Osprey were tested on synthetic data; every name and document is fictional. More on how review works inside them: Human-in-the-loop is a deliverable.
