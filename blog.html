What a €200 diagnosis actually finds — three recurring patterns
We changed how we start projects: a paid diagnosis first, a build only if it makes sense. After a run of them, the findings look remarkably similar from one business to the next.

Pattern one: the bottleneck is not where the owner thinks it is. People ask for a chatbot and turn out to be losing most of their week to re-typing data between two systems that never learned to talk to each other. The request describes the symptom; the map finds the cost.

Pattern two: the data is not ready. Automation inherits whatever discipline already exists. Inconsistent naming, three versions of the same price list, a spreadsheet nobody owns — none of that is an AI problem, and no model fixes it. Often the honest output of a diagnosis is a week of cleanup, not a build.

Pattern three: one process is worth automating, not seven. A diagnosis usually ends with a short list and a ranking, and the honest ranking is steep — the first item is worth several times the rest combined. Building that one thing well beats building four things halfway.

The diagnosis is deliberately paid and deliberately separate from the build. It removes the incentive to recommend work we would like to sell, and it leaves the client with a document they can take to anyone — including someone cheaper than us.

Building an invoice checking system in public — what the series taught us
We wrote up each stage of an invoice checking build as it happened — including the parts that did not work. Publishing before the outcome was known changed how we made decisions.

The uncomfortable part of building in public is that you commit to a design before you know whether it holds. Twice we published an approach and then had to publish its replacement. That is the point — a build log with no corrections in it is marketing, not a log.

It also shortened sales conversations to almost nothing. Clients who had read the series arrived knowing how we work, what we refuse to do, and where the limits are.

Practice · September 2026
Why we stopped scoring CVs and only parse them
Our CV tool used to rank candidates. Now it extracts fields into a structured table and stops there. The decision was part regulatory, part technical, and mostly about who should be doing the judging.

Scoring a candidate puts a system squarely inside employment decisions, which in the EU means a much heavier compliance regime — documentation, human oversight, bias testing, record-keeping. Parsing a document into fields does not.

The technical argument points the same way. Ranking quality is nearly impossible to validate honestly on small hiring volumes, while field extraction is checkable line by line. Recruiters keep the judgment; the system removes the typing.

Design · August 2026
Human-in-the-loop is a deliverable, not a safety blanket
Everyone says their workflow has human review. Very few can say who reviews, at which step, with what information on screen, and what happens to the queue when that person is on holiday.

A review step that is not designed becomes a rubber stamp within two weeks. People approve by default when the interface makes approving one click and questioning it five. So the design work is in the exception: what gets held back, what evidence is shown, and how long the queue may grow before someone is told.

Method · July 2026
Three questions we ask before writing a single node
Who notices when this breaks, how will we know it worked, and what does the business do on the day it is switched off? If any of the three has no answer, the automation is not ready to be built.

The third question is the one people dislike. An automation nobody can live without and nobody can operate is a liability dressed as an asset. We write the manual fallback down before we build, in the client’s own words, and we test it once.

The second question decides the price. If success cannot be measured, value-based pricing is guesswork and hourly billing is the honest option.
